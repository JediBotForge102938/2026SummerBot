Feedback control · Launcher velocity

A target speed is a request, not a guarantee. A controller repeatedly measures the launcher, compares it with the request, and adjusts motor effort. By the end, you should be able to explain P, I, D, and F, recognize their effects, and use Launcher Tuning to evaluate a change.

The control loop: request, measure, correct

Our launcher uses encoder ticks per second, not RPM. The encoder counts motor movement; velocity tells us how quickly those counts change. A target of 1200 ticks/s asks the motor to maintain that speed even when its load changes. Converting to RPM requires the correct encoder counts per revolution and any relevant gearing.

TermMeaningLauncher example
Target / setpointThe requested speed1200 ticks/s
Actual / measurementThe encoder's measured speed1140 ticks/s
ErrorTarget minus actual1200 - 1140 = +60 ticks/s
Control effortThe motor command used to correct speedMore or less electrical drive, within the controller's limits

Positive error means too slow; negative error means too fast. Closed-loop control uses the measurement to correct the result. In contrast, simply commanding a fixed motor power does not correct speed when battery voltage or load changes.

In LauncherSubsystem.java, we select RUN_USING_ENCODER, apply setPIDFCoefficients(...), and request speed with setVelocity(...). The Hub runs the motor's velocity controller; the OpMode changes its settings and displays the result. We are not implementing a separate PID loop in Java.

Describe the response before changing it

ObservationWhat to watch
Rise timeHow quickly speed approaches a new target after spin-up
OvershootHow far speed goes above the target before settling
Settling timeHow long it takes to enter and stay in the desired speed band
Steady-state errorThe persistent difference after the initial transient has settled
OscillationRepeated swings, rather than a stable speed with small measurement fluctuations
Disturbance recoveryHow quickly speed returns after a shot removes energy from the flywheel

Reading 1140 once during acceleration is different from holding near 1140 for ten seconds. Record the settled range, not only the highest reading. This OpMode displays live samples, not a plotted history or automatic response measurements; a teammate can record readings over time.

What response does each parameter adjust?

The letters name four contributions, combined into one motor command. This simplified equation explains the idea:

error = target - actual
effort = F * target
       + P * error
       + I * accumulated_error
       + D * rate_of_change_of_error

This is a conceptual model, not Java code to copy or an exact specification of Hub firmware. Controller scaling, sampling, output limits, and implementation details matter. A coefficient of 40 does not mean 40 percent power. Values from another robot, position controller, or software library are not automatically interchangeable.

ParameterResponds toMain response adjustedToo little / too much
P: proportionalError right nowImmediate correction strength; rise and recovery speedToo little: weak correction, sluggish recovery, possible offset. Too much: overshoot or repeated oscillation.
I: integralError accumulated over timeRemoval of persistent steady-state errorToo little: a residual offset may remain. Too much: delayed overshoot, slow oscillation, or prolonged settling.
D: derivativeHow quickly error changesDamping of rapid changes; overshoot and settling behaviorToo little: an otherwise underdamped response may keep overshooting. Too much: noisy corrections or sluggish response.
F: feedforwardRequested speed, without waiting for errorBaseline effort for the target; how much feedback correction is neededToo little: feedback must supply missing effort, possibly leaving an undershoot. Too much: feedback must counter excess effort, possibly causing overspeed.

P, I, and D are feedback; F is feedforward. Their effects interact. None exclusively controls one characteristic, and increasing a gain does not guarantee improvement.

P: correct the error now

At 1200 target and 1140 actual, P reacts to +60. If actual drops to 1080, the error becomes +120 and the proportional contribution doubles for the same P. Increasing P makes the same error produce a stronger correction. That can accelerate recovery, but an overly strong response can swing past the target and repeatedly correct in the opposite direction.

P alone does not guarantee zero steady-state error: with zero error, its contribution is zero. If F does not supply enough baseline effort, some positive error may remain to produce the needed P correction.

I: correct an error that keeps happening

If the launcher stays 60 ticks/s low, I remembers that continuing shortfall and builds additional correction. The same +60 error sustained for several seconds can produce more integral contribution than a brief +60 error. It can remove an offset that P and F leave behind.

Integral windup means accumulated correction becomes excessive while the motor cannot satisfy the request, such as during a large spin-up error or output saturation. When conditions improve, that stored correction can cause overshoot. Firmware may have protections, but do not assume they make a large I safe. Use the smallest useful I and check longer runs and restarts.

D: react to how the error is changing

Imagine two readings with the same +60 error. In one trial, speed is rising quickly toward target; in the other, it is stationary at 1140. P sees the same error, but D distinguishes the changing trend. In the conceptual model, rapidly shrinking positive error gives a negative derivative contribution that can temper the approach and reduce overshoot.

D is not a fix for a constant 60-ticks/s shortfall: constant error has approximately zero derivative. Encoder noise also looks like rapid change, so D can amplify it. Start this velocity-tuning exercise with D at zero; a stable response may not need D at all. A sudden target change can also affect derivative response, depending on the controller implementation.

F: supply the expected effort before an error develops

A flywheel needs effort to maintain speed even when it is exactly on target. F supplies a target-dependent baseline instead of making feedback discover all that effort through error. With a reasonably matched F, P/I/D can focus on correcting deviations.

A launcher that settles below each target but willingly spins faster when the target increases suggests useful speed headroom. An insufficient F or other tuning mismatch is plausible; it is not proof against battery, encoder, controller, or mechanical problems. Increase F in small trials, rather than raising the requested speed to disguise the error. With P active, do not assume multiplying F by target/actual gives the correct new coefficient.

Use Launcher Tuning

Hardware safety: work with an instructor, secure the robot, keep the launcher empty and the discharge area clear, and keep hands, hair, and loose clothing away from moving parts. STOP before inspecting hardware. Use only approved operating speeds; the software's 6000-ticks/s limit is not a safe-speed recommendation.
  1. Select the matching StarterBot or CompetitionBot hardware configuration, then Launcher Tuning under 2 - (Tests).
  2. Press INIT. Check Robot profile. The OpMode loads that profile's launcher motors, directions, and PIDF. Missing or invalid profiles stop initialization rather than guessing.
  3. Press START. Gamepad 1 D-pad up/down selects a row; left/right decreases/increases its value. Release between presses; held arrows do not repeat. Opposing arrows cancel.
  4. Hold RB to request spinning; release it to command zero speed. RB held across START must first be released. The flywheel may still coast after the command stops.
  5. Watch actual/target, signed error, and the tracking indicator. The competition gate stays closed; this OpMode does not run the intake or feeder.

Tracking is not the same as READY

At 1200 target, 1140 actual gives +60 error. That is OUTSIDE the tuning display's desired +/-50-ticks/s band, even if the launcher eventually reports READY.

IndicatorRuleWhat it tells us
Tracking (+/-50 ticks/s)WITHIN when absolute error is at most 50 and spinning is commanded; OFF otherwise when not commandedCurrent closeness to target on either side; no dwell requirement
ReadinessSpeed must stay strictly above target minus the profile margin for 200 ms to qualify; a lower dropout threshold adds hysteresis afterwardWhether the subsystem considers feeding available, not whether speed accurately tracks target

The observed competition setup uses a 125-ticks/s margin, a workaround for its undershoot; StarterBot uses 50. Check the displayed profile margin because saved or deployed settings can differ. At 1200, margins of 125 and 50 give initial qualification thresholds strictly above 1075 and 1150, respectively. Exactly 1150 can show WITHIN in this tuner but cannot initially qualify a 50-margin launcher. Readiness also has no upper-speed rejection: substantial overspeed can still be READY. Aim comfortably inside the tracking band, not exactly on an edge.

Predict, change one value, observe

Use a charged battery and one approved target for comparisons. Keep a driver ready to stop and a recorder watching telemetry. Do not deliberately create unstable behavior to demonstrate a parameter.

  1. Baseline: record the loaded P/I/D/F. The competition setup observed during this exercise used (40, 0, 0, 13.0); StarterBot used (40, 0, 0, 12.5). These are examples, not recommended final gains. Trust the displayed active profile if saved or deployed values differ. Spin for 5-10 seconds and record settled error and fluctuations.
  2. F trial: predict what a +0.1 F change will do to a sustained undershoot. Keep P fixed and I/D at zero for this initial exercise. Adjust F once, allow several seconds to settle, and record the result. Repeat small trials; switch to +0.01 near the desired band. Back off if speed goes too high.
  3. P trial: after F is reasonably matched, predict what +1 P will do to spin-up and recovery. Compare with the previous value. If repeated swings or excessive overshoot appear, reduce P rather than adding more gain. Keep the responsive, stable choice.
  4. I trial, only if needed: if a small stable offset remains, try +0.01 I or a finer step. Observe longer runs and stop/restart cycles. Keep it only if the offset improves without delayed overshoot or slow oscillation.
  5. D decision: explain why D will not remove a constant offset. Leave it zero if the response is stable. If overshoot remains after reducing P, an instructor can supervise a fine +0.01 D trial and check whether damping improves without noisy corrections.
  6. Compare targets: repeat with approved low, normal, and high shooting targets. Seek one useful coefficient set across the operating range, not a perfect reading at only 1200.

This sequence is a practical starting method, not a universal tuning recipe. Keep F fixed while comparing P, and so on; after a meaningful change, recheck earlier observations because the terms interact. If improvement stalls, review hardware and controller limits rather than endlessly increasing gains.

Keep and validate the result

STOP, then copy the final P/I/D/F into launcher.pidf in the matching robot-configs/biobuzz-competitionbot.profile.json or biobuzz-starterbot.profile.json. Run ./gradlew pushRobotConfigs with the intended Hub connected, and verify the same configuration is active. See the launcher tuning reference and configuration upload guide.

With an instructor, use normal TeleOp to validate shooting and recovery, since this tuner cannot feed. Repeat across normal battery conditions. Once tracking reliably supports it, restore the competition profile's feedVelocityMarginTicksPerSecond to the desired 50 and verify feeding and dropout behavior. That margin is a separate policy setting, not a PIDF gain.

Know the limits: no gain can create unavailable voltage or torque. Persistent problems may involve battery sag, output saturation, friction, load, motor choice, wiring, or encoder/motor configuration. The simulator can teach controls and code flow, but its idealized motor behavior cannot establish physical robot PIDF values.

Quick retrieval check

  1. The launcher settles at 1140 with a target of 1200. What is the signed error, and is it inside +/-50?
  2. Which term reacts immediately to current error? Which reacts to a shortfall that persists?
  3. Why can increasing P improve recovery but also increase oscillation?
  4. Why does D distinguish a quickly shrinking error from an unchanged error of the same size?
  5. Why does a flywheel need F even when the error is zero?
  6. Can READY certify accurate tracking? Does increasing the readiness margin fix control?
  7. Where do you save the tuned gains, and what must be tested outside this OpMode?
